Reconstructing My Apartment in 3D with VGGT-SLAM

VGGT-SLAM Monocular SLAM Dense Reconstruction Loop Closure Submap Alignment Phone Video

The Premise

I was moving out. Instead of taking a last photo of the place, I wanted the whole apartment as geometry — something you can fly a camera through rather than look at from one angle.

The constraint I set was to use nothing but what was in my pocket: one phone video, walked through the rooms. No LiDAR, no depth sensor, no calibration target, no photogrammetry turntable ritual. If it needed equipment, it did not count.

Why VGGT-SLAM

VGGT-SLAM (MIT-SPARK) is dense RGB SLAM built on VGGT, the feed-forward geometry transformer. Rather than tracking sparse features and triangulating over time, it predicts dense geometry for chunks of frames directly, builds the scene as a series of submaps, and stitches them together — optimising the alignment on the SL(4) manifold, so the submaps are related by homographies rather than rigid transforms. It takes ordinary monocular video, which is exactly the input I had.

First Attempt: 60 Seconds of Footage

I started with the first minute of the walkthrough, on the reasonable assumption that a shorter clip would be a faster way to find out whether this would work at all.

The result was junk. Floating slabs — fragments of wall and floor hanging in space at plausible-looking angles that did not join into anything. Not a bad reconstruction of a room; not a room at all.

🎯 The instinct to debug the model was wrong. Nothing was wrong with the geometry prediction. The problem was that the clip never gave the system a chance to recognise where it had already been.

What Fixed It: The Full Three Minutes

I fed in the complete three-minute video — the same footage, same camera, same apartment, just not truncated. It snapped into an actual floor plan.

The difference was loop closures:

  • 1-minute clip: 2 loop closures.
  • Full clip: 10 loop closures.

Walking past the same hallway a second time is what does it. When the system recognises a place it has already mapped, that recognition becomes a constraint, and the optimiser can pull everything either side of it back into agreement — in this run, 39 drifting submaps dragged back into alignment. That is the step that turned debris into a map.

The lesson generalises beyond this project: with drift-prone reconstruction, a longer capture is not merely more data, it is more constraints. Trimming the clip to iterate faster removed the exact thing the method depends on.

What Still Does Not Work

I panned more than I walked. Sweeping the phone around from a standing position produces rotation without translation, and rotation alone carries no parallax — nothing for the geometry to triangulate against. The areas I captured by turning on the spot are the weakest parts of the reconstruction. A slower walk covering the same ground would beat a fast pan every time.

Monocular means no absolute scale. The reconstruction is correct in shape and proportion but it is not measurable: nothing in a single RGB stream establishes what one unit is in metres. To get metric scale you have to bring something in from outside — a known object in frame, IMU data, or a stereo pair. As it stands it is the right apartment, just not one you could order furniture from.

Takeaways

  1. Capture quality decides the result more than the model does. Same model, same scene, same phone — the only variable was how much of the walk it was given, and it was the difference between debris and a floor plan.
  2. Revisit your path on purpose. Loop closures are not a bonus; they are the mechanism that holds a long capture together. Walking the hallway twice is a deliberate act of data collection.
  3. Translate, do not pan. Parallax is what monocular reconstruction eats. A phone rotating on the spot supplies almost none of it.
  4. Know which properties you are not getting. Shape without scale is still useful, as long as nobody downstream assumes the units mean something.
